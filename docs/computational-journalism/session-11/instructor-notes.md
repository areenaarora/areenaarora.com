# Session 11 · Instructor notes

Writing What the Data Can Defend

70 minutes: lecture 0–30; guided analysis 30–50; editorial workshop 50–70. Slide 31 is a reference within slide 30’s task time. Slide 34 is homework. Press N for notes in the deck; Escape closes the dialog. No notes link or section label clutters the slide canvas.

The instructor-only case-study answer key is a separate repository document. It is not embedded in the student deck.

## 01 · Writing What the Data Can Defend

00:00–00:30 · 0.5 min

**Central argument:** Evidence sets the ceiling for a claim.

**Concept:** The task is epistemic calibration, not elementary prose style.

**Example:** Students bring already published data stories.

**Say / demonstrate:** Connect to their submitted sites. Today’s standard is the strongest consequential statement the evidence warrants, not the loudest possible interpretation.

**Ask:** Which sentence in your own story would you least want an editor to challenge?

**Defensible answers:** Any specific sentence is useful; preserve the question for the homework.

**Common misconception:** A more cautious-sounding sentence is necessarily a more rigorous one. Precision can support forceful conclusions.

**Transition:** Start with two accurate summaries that create different impressions.

## 02 · The numbers can be right

00:30–01:45 · 1.25 min

**Central argument:** Accuracy at the level of individual numbers does not guarantee an accurate overall impression.

**Concept:** Estimand choice: absolute burden and population-standardised risk answer different questions.

**Example:** 100/10,000 × 1,000 = 10; 120/15,000 × 1,000 = 8. Counts grow 20%; the rate falls 20%.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to defend both statements. Demand the reporting question before choosing the lead. Check that coverage, case definitions and exposure are comparable.

**Ask:** Which account would you lead with for hospital capacity? For an individual’s risk?

**Defensible answers:** Capacity can justify the count; resident risk may justify the rate, if population is the relevant exposure. Both may belong in the story.

**Common misconception:** Rates are always more truthful than counts, or one of these statements must be mathematically false.

**Transition:** Separate what the data shows from the explanation we attach to it.

## 03 · Analysis is not argument

01:45–03:15 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Observation, mechanism and publishable claim are distinct.

**Concept:** A mean is a ratio; composition, user entry and payment substitution are different explanations.

**Example:** ₹10bn/100m = ₹100; ₹9bn/150m = ₹60. No cash-use observations appear in the example.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to list competing explanations: a change in merchant mix, user mix, ticket sizes or large transfers. Cash substitution needs evidence about the alternative channel and preferably the same people over time.

**Ask:** What would you request before writing the cash sentence?

**Defensible answers:** Representative payment diaries or household/merchant surveys, transaction composition, coverage over time, and evidence of substitution rather than additional transactions.

**Common misconception:** An observed fall in the mean identifies its cause. “May” can flag a hypothesis but does not establish it.

**Transition:** Rank different kinds of claims by what they require.

## 04 · The ladder of claims

03:15–05:45 · 2.5 min

**Central argument:** Stronger claims change the evidence requirement; they are not merely stronger prose.

**Concept:** Description, comparison, association, mechanism, causal identification, prediction and normative policy choice are different inferential tasks.

**Example:** Programme participants: 40% employed before, 60% after. Comparison group: 30% before, 50% after.

**Say / demonstrate:** Move through the rungs. A plausible mechanism can support an explanation without estimating an effect. Causal evidence needs a credible counterfactual and assumptions. Prescription adds costs, alternatives and values; it is not mechanically the final rung.

**Ask:** What changes when “rose” becomes “was caused by training”?

**Defensible answers:** We must estimate what would have happened without training, address selection and common shocks, and justify identification assumptions.

**Common misconception:** A larger sample upgrades association to causation. Prediction is not automatically causation; prescription also requires explicit objectives and trade-offs.

**Transition:** Audit the verbs that quietly move a story up the ladder.

## 05 · Verbs carry a burden of proof

05:45–07:00 · 1.25 min

**Central argument:** Verbs encode commitments about relationships.

**Concept:** Calibration of causal language and the evidential status of hypotheses.

**Example:** Training and employment rise together; that supports neither “drove” nor a causal forecast by itself.

**Say / demonstrate:** Have students underline the verb in a draft. “Contributed to” is not a harmless descriptive fallback. Qualifiers should communicate uncertainty in an evidence-backed claim, not disguise missing support.

**Ask:** Can “could have caused” ever be useful?

**Defensible answers:** As an explicitly identified hypothesis, with a reason to consider it and alternatives; not as a substitute for causal evidence in the finding.

**Common misconception:** Every causal verb requires a randomised trial, or adding “may” fixes an unsupported causal claim. Credible non-experimental designs exist.

**Transition:** Put the proposed explanation through a counterfactual test.

## 06 · The counterfactual test

07:00–09:15 · 2.25 min

**Central argument:** A before/after increase is compatible with no programme effect.

**Concept:** Difference-in-differences intuition, common shocks, confounding, selection and parallel-trends assumptions.

**Example:** The naive before/after gain is 20 percentage points; the illustrative difference in changes is zero.

**Say / demonstrate:** Use a recovering local labour market as a competing explanation, not a fact supplied by the table. The contrast is a diagnostic, not an automatic causal estimate. Ask about pre-trends, cohort composition, spillovers, differential shocks and programme selection.

**Ask:** Does zero difference in changes prove the programme had no effect?

**Defensible answers:** No. Identification assumptions, measurement and uncertainty matter; effects can differ by group or period. The table undermines the naive causal claim without proving exact zero.

**Common misconception:** An untreated group automatically supplies the counterfactual; “after” establishes “because”; a zero estimate proves no effect.

**Transition:** The same discipline applies to choices made before the first chart.

## 07 · Seven decisions behind every story

09:15–10:30 · 1.25 min

**Central argument:** Editorial perspective is unavoidable; arbitrary and concealed choices are avoidable.

**Concept:** Transparency, robustness and analytical degrees of freedom.

**Example:** An “employment” story can study registered formal employees or all working-age adults; neither file chooses the reporting question.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to name one decision they made in their submitted story. The standard is whether a reasonable alternative changes the result, and whether the choice is defensible for the question.

**Ask:** Which choice could reverse your own headline?

**Defensible answers:** Plausible answers include sample exclusions, population denominator, baseline or subgroup weighting. Ask for a concrete sensitivity check.

**Common misconception:** Objectivity means having made no choices. Revealing a choice does not by itself justify it.

**Transition:** Test one of the most consequential choices numerically.

## 08 · The denominator changes the question

10:30–12:00 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** There is no universally correct denominator independent of the question.

**Concept:** Population at risk, exposure time, standardisation and capacity burden.

**Example:** The same example as slide 2, now with every denominator visible.

**Say / demonstrate:** Compute the two rates aloud. For traffic injuries, trips or vehicle-kilometres may be more relevant than residents. For workplace harm, worker-hours may matter. Age-standardisation can be necessary when age composition changes.

**Ask:** If hospital capacity is fixed, can a falling resident rate coexist with overload?

**Defensible answers:** Yes: more cases can create more demand even as the population-standardised rate falls. Need severity, case mix and capacity evidence.

**Common misconception:** Always use per-capita rates; use the entire population even when only a subgroup is exposed.

**Transition:** Now keep the denominator fixed and change the period.

## 09 · The timeframe is an editorial decision

12:00–13:30 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Period selection needs an editorial rationale and robustness checks.

**Concept:** Base effects, structural breaks, seasonality, data revisions and selecting on outcomes.

**Example:** Index values: 100, 102, 106, 70, 85, 105, 110, 112, 114. Window changes yield +14%, +62.9% and −0.9%.

**Say / demonstrate:** Toggle the windows. A disruption year can be appropriate for measuring recovery but not necessarily long-run improvement. Establish the question and period before seeing the preferred answer. Keep the longer context visible and record the data release. Annual data cannot diagnose monthly seasonality.

**Ask:** When is starting at the trough legitimate?

**Defensible answers:** When recovery from that documented disruption is the question, with the baseline explained and the longer view available. It is misleading if chosen to imply ordinary growth.

**Common misconception:** Every unusual baseline is forbidden; annual trends rule out seasonal effects; revisions cannot change earlier comparisons.

**Transition:** Ask whose observations the time series contains.

## 10 · Selection bias and invisible observations

13:30–15:00 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Missingness can depend on the outcome and reverse the apparent story.

**Concept:** Attrition, survivor selection and missing-not-at-random mechanisms.

**Example:** (60 × 75 + 40 × 20) / 100 = 53, below the initial 60 despite the returning group’s 75.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask why pupils might be absent. We do not know their scores. The hypothetical value demonstrates non-identification; it is not a justified imputation. Report the observed group and missing share, then seek follow-up evidence.

**Ask:** What if missing pupils were the strongest students?

**Defensible answers:** The bias can run in the opposite direction. Investigate the missingness process; bounds or sensitivity analysis can be more honest than a single adjusted number.

**Common misconception:** Unobserved people can safely be assigned zero; a higher mean among respondents proves cohort improvement.

**Transition:** Even complete records can be incomparable.

## 11 · Comparability is not a footnote

15:00–16:30 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Arithmetic cannot repair incompatible constructs and populations.

**Concept:** Measurement validity, target-population alignment and comparability.

**Example:** The two displayed amounts are not alternative estimates of the same quantity.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask what transformations are possible and which mismatches remain. Annual-to-monthly division is straightforward; household-to-person conversion needs household information and proper weights. Inflation adjustment needs an appropriate index. A registry census is not necessarily coverage of the entire population.

**Ask:** After dividing B by 12, are the figures comparable?

**Defensible answers:** No. The construct, household unit, coverage, geography, prices and years still differ. A precise conversion can leave an invalid comparison.

**Common misconception:** A common currency or date makes income and consumption identical; a census of registered units is a population census.

**Transition:** Now compare distributions with identical means.

## 12 · Averages conceal distributions

16:30–18:30 · 2 min

**Central argument:** A summary statistic does not specify the distribution or typical experience.

**Concept:** Means, medians, dispersion, within-group heterogeneity and quantile definitions.

**Example:** Both sums are 50. The second distribution has four low values and one large value.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to calculate the means and medians. A percentile is a rank position; its cutoff is a value on the welfare axis. A mean for people above that cutoff requires their values or a valid grouped-data estimate. Different sample-quantile conventions exist, so do not estimate P90 from these five points as if unique.

**Ask:** Can the same mean coexist with most people having less than the mean?

**Defensible answers:** Yes: four of five values in B are 1 while the mean is 10. A national mean does not describe everyone, and the same rate can conceal different counts.

**Common misconception:** The 90th-percentile cutoff is the average income of the top decile; the 90th percentile means 90% of the mean; a group average describes every member.

**Transition:** Changing group weights can even reverse a pooled trend.

## 13 · Aggregation changes the question

18:30–20:30 · 2 min

**Central argument:** Aggregation changes the weights and sometimes the direction of a comparison.

**Concept:** Simpson’s paradox and ecological inference.

**Example:** A’s rate drops 10 points; B’s drops 10 points. Yet the pooled rate rises about 30.5 points because the mix shifts toward A.

**Say / demonstrate:** Calculate the totals rather than averaging district percentages. The pooled figure answers a real question about the observed aggregate. It does not identify an improvement within either district. An area-level association also need not hold for individuals within it: ecological fallacy is a related but distinct problem.

**Ask:** Which summary would you publish?

**Defensible answers:** Depends on the question: give the pooled burden with the composition explanation, and within-district rates for performance. Standardise weights if that is the estimand, stating why.

**Common misconception:** The aggregate is necessarily wrong, or Simpson’s paradox and ecological fallacy are the same thing.

**Transition:** Even an aligned comparison may be uncertain.

## 14 · Uncertainty is part of the finding

20:30–22:00 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Uncertainty qualifies what can be distinguished, not whether a story is worth reporting.

**Concept:** Sampling intervals, meaningful effect sizes, equivalence and non-sampling error.

**Example:** The stated interval includes zero and effects in either direction; it is not contained in an illustrative equivalence margin of ±0.5 points.

**Say / demonstrate:** Explain that a frequentist 95% interval concerns repeated-procedure coverage under its assumptions. It is not a 95% probability assigned to this fixed parameter. Registry coverage errors, missingness, revisions and model assumptions need separate checks. A large administrative file may have little sampling error but substantial measurement bias.

**Ask:** What evidence would support “no meaningful difference”?

**Defensible answers:** A predefined meaningful margin and an interval or equivalence analysis narrow enough to exclude effects outside it, with sound measurement and design.

**Common misconception:** Non-significance proves equality; all datasets should receive the same confidence interval; a sample of millions removes measurement error.

**Transition:** A literal zero in a register is another form of uncertainty.

## 15 · Absence of evidence

22:00–23:15 · 1.25 min

**Central argument:** Zero recorded events is not necessarily zero underlying events.

**Concept:** Detection processes, reporting probabilities and the difference between absence of evidence and evidence of absence.

**Example:** A workplace complaints register with zero recorded entries.

**Say / demonstrate:** Give students 20 seconds to rewrite the claim. A reliable exhaustive system with high detection can make a zero informative. Without that information, investigate reporting access, retaliation, retention and independent accounts.

**Ask:** What would make the zero persuasive evidence of very low incidence?

**Defensible answers:** Strong coverage, a functioning independent reporting or audit system, known detection properties and corroboration; still define the time and population.

**Common misconception:** Every zero is meaningless, or a blank field is the same as a verified zero. A zero can be strong evidence under suitable measurement.

**Transition:** Carry those distinctions into the architecture of a story.

## 16 · Argument, not a tour of charts

23:15–24:30 · 1.25 min

**Central argument:** An argument relates evidence to a claim and anticipates objections.

**Concept:** Claim–evidence–warrant reasoning and disconfirming evidence.

**Example:** The trainees’ gain is real, while the comparison group’s gain complicates a programme-success explanation.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask which chart most threatens the preferred claim and move it into the story, not a hidden appendix. Explanatory reporting can include interviews and documents but must be weighed, not merely appended. Significance is a question of consequences, not dramatic phrasing.

**Ask:** Where does the strongest counterexample belong?

**Defensible answers:** Near the claim it qualifies, early enough to shape the reader’s interpretation. Later method notes cannot fully undo an overclaiming lead.

**Common misconception:** The reporting process must determine the published sequence; a story with an argument is advocacy by definition.

**Transition:** Test three ledes against the same evidence.

## 17 · The strongest defensible lede

24:30–26:30 · 2 min

**Central argument:** A precise lede can retain energy while narrowing an unsupported inference.

**Concept:** Scope, comparison and calibration of causal claims.

**Example:** A invents a causal result and a policy mandate; B makes readers do the argument; C states the material change and counterevidence.

**Say / demonstrate:** Let students improve C, rather than presenting it as perfect. “Surveyed” names the observed population. “No faster” refers to equal 20-point changes, not equal final employment levels. If estimates have uncertainty, the equivalence wording needs further care; this exact fictional table is arithmetic, not a statistical equivalence test.

**Ask:** What additional reporting could make a stronger programme claim fair?

**Defensible answers:** A credible impact evaluation, pre-trends and assignment information, mechanisms, alternative outcomes, costs and who benefits.

**Common misconception:** A caveat at the end repairs any dramatic first sentence; matching changes means final levels are the same.

**Transition:** Clear conclusions do not require false balance.

## 18 · Fairness is not false balance

26:30–28:00 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Fairness requires evidential proportionality, not automatic symmetry.

**Concept:** Normative commitments versus methods of verification; appropriate weighting of competing accounts.

**Example:** An illustrative audit documents failures in 80 of 100 inspected sites while an official denies any failures.

**Say / demonstrate:** Give the official a fair opportunity to respond and assess their evidence. Do not turn a substantiated result into a he-said/she-said stalemate. Check whether inspected sites represent all sites before generalising. The terms on the slide are working distinctions, not a universal taxonomy.

**Ask:** Does a forceful conclusion violate fairness?

**Defensible answers:** No, if scope and strength track the evidence, material counterevidence is addressed and relevant people are fairly represented.

**Common misconception:** Objectivity means indecision; fairness means a contrary quote after every chart; transparency alone proves validity.

**Transition:** Adopt the posture of an editor who wants the claim to survive scrutiny.

## 19 · The adversarial editor

28:00–29:30 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** Adversarial editing protects the story from overclaiming.

**Concept:** Falsifiability, rival explanations, construct validity and transparent warrants.

**Example:** Apply all five tests to the training claim, not to five unrelated hypothetical stories.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to choose the most consequential missing evidence and phrase it as a reporting request. The editor is trying to strengthen a defensible story, not destroy its news value.

**Ask:** What observation would force you to change the lead?

**Defensible answers:** A credible comparison indicating the same change without treatment, measurement changes, programme selection, or contrary outcomes; specify the mechanism of the challenge.

**Common misconception:** A sufficiently sceptical editor never publishes; collecting another supportive quote is an adversarial test.

**Transition:** Take these questions into a real article.

## 20 · The governing principle

29:30–30:00 · 0.5 min

**Central argument:** The goal is disciplined ambition rather than maximal or timid claims.

**Concept:** Calibrated confidence and the boundary between observation and interpretation.

**Example:** The next section applies the same tests to a published article.

**Say / demonstrate:** Close the lecture at 30 minutes. Do not preview the case-study critique. Invite students to interrogate the headline before seeing the reconstruction.

**Ask:** What is the difference between a weak story and a bounded claim?

**Defensible answers:** A bounded claim can be consequential and well supported. A weak story may lack evidence or public relevance, regardless of confidence in its tone.

**Common misconception:** Precision drains all narrative power.

**Transition:** Read the published headline on its own terms.

## 21 · The published headline

30:00–31:30 · 1.5 min

**Central argument:** First reconstruct the claim before evaluating it.

**Concept:** Headline semantics: population, measure, group comparison and attribution.

**Example:** The headline is accurately typeset from the article as retrieved on 20 October 2026; it is not a fabricated screenshot.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students what “earn,” “richest” and a comparison between groups would ordinarily imply. Do not reveal the answer key or subsequent calculations yet. Separate what the headline says from what a student assumes the dataset contains.

**Ask:** Is the headline about cutoffs, averages, typical people, or every member?

**Defensible answers:** Students may read it as a group-level or typical-person comparison. The wording is ambiguous; record those readings rather than declaring a single mathematical proposition.

**Common misconception:** Every reader interprets a group comparison identically; an article’s attribution means the institution wrote the headline.

**Transition:** Read the whole article before judging its evidence.

## 22 · Read like an editor

31:30–34:30 · 3 min

**Central argument:** Distinguish reported statistics, source interpretation and policy recommendations.

**Concept:** Source criticism and provenance of each step in a published argument.

**Example:** Use the original article, not a slide summary or its automated quick-read panel.

**Say / demonstrate:** Allow silent reading. Ask students to label a sentence’s evidential role, not whether they agree with its politics. Notice where a descriptive distribution comparison becomes an explanation or a recommendation.

**Ask:** Which sentence requires a different kind of evidence than the numerical comparison?

**Defensible answers:** An explanation or policy recommendation requires evidence beyond the distribution table. Attribution makes an expert opinion visible but does not validate it.

**Common misconception:** The assignment is to find as many errors as possible; a quoted interpretation must be either an established fact or a lie.

**Transition:** Rebuild the source comparison from its underlying records.

## 23 · Reconstruct the comparison

34:30–37:30 · 3 min

**Central argument:** A percentile threshold locates a boundary, not the average of the adjacent group.

**Concept:** Weighted quantiles, survey reference years and the distinction between population ranks and value scales.

**Example:** The displayed P10 and P90 values come from national quantile rows in the retained 2021-PPP extract. Both PPP vintages were inspected.

**Say / demonstrate:** Explain the shaded population shares. India’s upper decile begins above its P90; the US lower decile lies below its P10. The strips are percentile ranks, not density curves. The table documents all four comparators without pretending their survey years are contemporaneous.

**Ask:** Where are the values for people inside the shaded groups?

**Defensible answers:** The boundaries do not provide those values. Group means need the distribution above or below the cutoff; the source also supplies bin averages and population shares.

**Common misconception:** A 2026 dataset release means all observations were collected in 2026; the two shaded blocks have the same monetary width.

**Transition:** Test exactly what follows from ordering those boundaries.

## 24 · The crucial distinction

37:30–40:30 · 3 min

**Central argument:** Reject an invalid inference without automatically declaring its conclusion false.

**Concept:** Logical implication versus an independently established result; subgroup averages and individual overlap.

**Example:** A hypothetical P90 of 10 and P10 of 25 permit a top-decile mean of 1,000 and a bottom-decile mean of 20; the boundary inequality alone does not determine means. Separately, the actual selected bins produce the displayed cross-measure averages.

**Say / demonstrate:** Give students a counterexample before revealing the actual means. For the source calculation, weight each percentile-bin mean by pop_share across bins 91–100 or 1–10. The tenth bin’s mean is not the entire bottom decile’s mean. The four comparator lower-decile means are all above the selected Indian upper-decile consumption mean; see the answer key.

**Ask:** So is the published comparison mathematically disproved?

**Defensible answers:** No. Its inference from thresholds would be invalid, but a separate mean calculation in this extract preserves the numerical ordering. Earnings wording, mixed measures, ambiguous group meaning, survey years and coverage still need editing.

**Common misconception:** If an argument is invalid its conclusion must be false; if group means are ordered every individual in one group is poorer than every individual in the other.

**Transition:** Ask what those two welfare measures actually capture.

## 25 · Income is not consumption

40:30–42:30 · 2 min

**Central argument:** The exact welfare construct changes what a journalist may call the result.

**Concept:** Income flows versus consumption, smoothing, household allocation and person weighting.

**Example:** A hypothetical household with temporarily low earnings but consumption financed by savings demonstrates why consumption is not a synonym for wages.

**Say / demonstrate:** The selected India record is consumption; all four comparator records are income. PIP’s per-capita welfare allocates household aggregates across household members for measurement. That does not imply each member personally earns or controls the reported amount. Cross-concept comparisons can be informative, but need explicit naming and cannot be relabeled as pay.

**Ask:** Would replacing “earn” with “live on” solve every comparability problem?

**Defensible answers:** No. It may improve construct alignment but still requires disclosure of mixed measures, years, PPP, source construction and what a group statistic means.

**Common misconception:** Income and consumption are unrelated; consumption measures only cash purchases; household per-person income equals each person’s labour earnings.

**Transition:** Build a compact comparability audit rather than an automatic rejection.

## 26 · Are these numbers comparable?

42:30–45:00 · 2.5 min

**Central argument:** Comparability is a graded, documented judgment rather than a yes/no currency check.

**Concept:** PPP reference year versus welfare reference year, survey frame, common units and remaining measurement differences.

**Example:** The matching Australian record is 2020; the others are 2022. Later Indian and US years exist in the file, so this is a reconstruction of selected rows rather than a latest-observation comparison.

**Say / demonstrate:** Distinguish a PPP price reference year, a survey/welfare year, the year the interview took place, and a database release date. A national label does not guarantee inclusion of every person. Survey nonresponse and non-household populations can affect the tails; do not quantify a correction without evidence.

**Ask:** Must we abandon every cross-country comparison with mixed welfare concepts?

**Defensible answers:** No. State the constructs, purpose and sensitivity; narrow the claim to the evidence. A robust broad living-standard contrast may be useful while an exact wage or wealth ranking is unsupported.

**Common misconception:** PPP dollars are US banknotes converted at today’s exchange rate; a national household sample observes all wealthy or unhoused residents.

**Transition:** Now inspect a rupee figure with a different unit and an incomplete citation.

## 27 · The ₹80,000 comparison

45:00–47:00 · 2 min

**Central argument:** An unexplained unit and source switch cannot corroborate a percentile comparison.

**Concept:** Earnings versus household consumption, nominal versus PPP units and the population ranked.

**Example:** Only the short excerpt is quoted verbatim. The official 2025–26 report was searched; no matching personal-earnings percentile statement was located. This search does not establish what every earlier edition contains.

**Say / demonstrate:** Do not convert the consumption threshold to rupees with the market exchange rate. Even a correct CPI/PPP/month conversion would not turn consumption into an individual earnings distribution. Ask whether the figure is a cutoff or mean and whether the ranked population is workers, earners, adults or all people.

**Ask:** What exactly do you send back to the reporter?

**Defensible answers:** Please supply the Economic Survey edition, page/table, underlying dataset, period, definition of earnings, ranked population and statistic. Then explain how it relates to the PIP measure.

**Common misconception:** Failure to locate a citation proves fabrication; dividing or multiplying by 30 and an exchange rate solves the conceptual mismatch.

**Transition:** State the strongest conclusion and the boundary around it.

## 28 · The strongest fair conclusion

47:00–50:00 · 3 min

**Central argument:** A fair conclusion is neither maximalist nor emptied of public significance.

**Concept:** Separate descriptive evidence, interpretation, non-identification and normative or causal policy claims.

**Example:** The cutoff and independently computed mean orderings support a measured welfare contrast; they do not identify why it arose or which reform package would change it.

**Say / demonstrate:** Ask students to formulate a conclusion before revealing the four categories. Expert recommendations can be newsworthy when clearly attributed, with evidence and alternatives evaluated. The data does not itself issue a policy prescription.

**Ask:** What should survive an edit of the article?

**Defensible answers:** The substantial measured contrast and its public relevance. Name the chosen statistic and mixed welfare concepts; avoid claims about all individuals or causal policy effects. Multiple ledes can be defensible.

**Common misconception:** Correcting framing requires discarding the finding, or naming limitations requires treating every interpretation as equally plausible.

**Transition:** Give groups the editorial deadline and ask them to produce publishable work.

## 29 · You’re the editor

50:00–51:00 · 1 min

**Central argument:** Editing must preserve warranted news value while repairing the reasoning.

**Concept:** Evidence-preserving editorial intervention.

**Example:** The class has the article, definitions, selected source rows and verification statuses.

**Say / demonstrate:** Assign an editor, source checker and drafter; a fourth student can test the rewrite against the headline. The 20-minute block includes task setup, drafting and comparison. At minute 64 transition to sharing.

**Ask:** Which correction is essential before publication?

**Defensible answers:** Examples include defining the statistic, replacing an earnings claim when the underlying welfare measure is consumption, and clearly distinguishing verified facts from unresolved citations.

**Common misconception:** This is an exercise in making the story politically neutral or replacing it with an unrelated story.

**Transition:** Make the deliverables concrete.

## 30 · Your assignment

51:00–64:00 · 13 min

**Central argument:** Students must make and defend an integrated editorial judgment.

**Concept:** Synthesis of construct validity, inferential scope and narrative priorities.

**Example:** Use the actual source table; do not invent a different dataset or fill the unresolved citation with a guess.

**Say / demonstrate:** Allow 13 minutes. Suggest: 3 minutes diagnosis, 7 minutes drafting, 3 minutes checking. Point groups to the following constraints slide during the same period; it adds no lecture time. A 120-word lede is deliberately longer than a conventional one-sentence lead so it can include finding, scope and qualification.

**Ask:** Which sentence contains your strongest claim, and which source supports it?

**Defensible answers:** Require a traceable source and a correct statistic. Allow varied emphases and clear editorial choices rather than a single approved phrasing.

**Common misconception:** The task rewards counting minor wording flaws; caveats can replace the finding; “approximately 120” requires exactly 120 words.

**Transition:** Use the constraints as a checklist, then compare drafts.

## 31 · Editorial constraints

Within slide 30’s 13-minute task · no extra time

**Central argument:** Constraints protect meaning while leaving room for editorial craft.

**Concept:** Boundary conditions of evidence-preserving rewriting.

**Example:** Use the source extract, rather than a made-up alternative value, when explaining a statistic.

**Say / demonstrate:** Keep this visible while groups work within slide 30’s 13 minutes. Do not add a second task period. Remind students that separately computed group means are available, but must be identified as a new calculation with mixed measures.

**Ask:** Are you qualifying a supported finding, or disguising an unsupported one?

**Defensible answers:** A supported finding names what the data measures and limits its scope. An unsupported finding remains unsupported after adding vague modifiers.

**Common misconception:** Every qualifier is a virtue; any forceful verb is bias; constraints imply one perfect headline.

**Transition:** Invite selected groups to share, with the original evidence still available.

## 32 · Compare your edits

64:00–69:00 · 5 min

**Central argument:** Evaluate revisions by evidential integrity and communication, not caution alone.

**Concept:** Editorial trade-offs and peer review.

**Example:** Two temporary text areas support two group comparisons; a third group can be read aloud or replace one after discussion.

**Say / demonstrate:** Choose two groups at roughly two minutes each and one minute to compare. With three groups, shorten presentations. Ask students to identify the precise trade-off rather than vote on style. Text is neither saved nor submitted. Clear only after copying anything the instructor wants to retain.

**Ask:** Which version would you publish, and what evidence makes it defensible?

**Defensible answers:** No single answer is hard-coded. A good answer preserves the finding, correctly describes the statistic, separates income from consumption and places limits where readers need them.

**Common misconception:** The best edit is the most cautious, the longest, or the one with the most caveats.

**Transition:** End with precision as a condition of credibility.

## 33 · Final lesson

69:00–70:00 · 1 min

**Central argument:** Precision increases the credibility of consequential writing.

**Concept:** Confidence proportional to evidence, with explicit scope and honest uncertainty.

**Example:** Return to one strong student sentence that survived the editorial tests.

**Say / demonstrate:** Finish the in-class block at minute 70. Emphasise the methodological choices that made a sentence more powerful, not merely less dramatic. Then set the next slide as homework, with no new timed activity.

**Ask:** Which change made the story stronger rather than simply safer?

**Defensible answers:** A better comparison, correct welfare construct, a clarified statistic or an independently supported explanation can sharpen significance.

**Common misconception:** A bold conclusion and careful statistical reasoning are incompatible.

**Transition:** Assign the same adversarial review to each student’s own published story.

## 34 · Put your own story on trial

Homework · outside the 70-minute class

**Central argument:** Transfer the critique to the student’s own reporting.

**Concept:** Reflexivity, source traceability and epistemic limits.

**Example:** Audit the previously submitted course website; this is not a new reporting assignment.

**Say / demonstrate:** Assign outside class. Ask for a precise original sentence and its proposed replacement, source identification and one newly recognised limit. Do not make this another in-class exercise.

**Ask:** What important claim will you now choose not to make?

**Defensible answers:** A useful answer identifies a concrete unsupported inference rather than a general statement that all data is imperfect.

**Common misconception:** The memo must confess an error even if the original claim is defensible. It can defend choices while identifying remaining limits.

**Transition:** Carry the editorial standard into subsequent reporting.
