# Session 12 — Instructor notes

75 minutes · ACJ Computational Journalism capstone



## 1. Your work, your website, your next move

00:00–01:00 · 1 min



**Objective:** Frame the final class as editorial work, not a job-placement promise.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** You already have reporting, analysis and publishing tools. Today you will edit how your work presents you, publish a first version and choose a sustainable next step. This is the final ACJ Computational Journalism class. A portfolio is an edited argument about the journalist you are becoming.

**Ask:** What should an editor remember after leaving your site?

**Expected responses:** A specific reporting interest supported by actual work, not a list of adjectives or languages.

**Misconception:** A polished portfolio guarantees a job. It makes evidence legible; it cannot guarantee hiring.

**Transition:** Give the portfolio a real reader.



## 2. The hiring editor’s 15 minutes

01:00–04:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Treat the portfolio as an editorial product with an audience and a reading order.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** This is a constructed scenario, not a measured recruitment benchmark. An editor scanning fifty applications is allocating scarce attention. Identity, a strong project and evidence of responsibility should appear before ornamental introductions. Ask students to name what they would look for if hiring a local government reporter versus a graphics journalist.

**Ask:** Would your homepage answer those three questions before a visitor scrolls?

**Expected responses:** A short identity line, selected work with concrete findings and role/method links help. A dramatic animation or generic biography delays the evidence.

**Misconception:** Editors all want the same portfolio. The audience and role shape the selection; clarity still matters.

**Transition:** Compare an archive with a claim.



## 3. Your portfolio is an argument

04:00–06:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Make curation an argument supported by work.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** The thesis is: a portfolio is not an archive of everything you have made; it is an edited argument about the journalist you are becoming. Three projects can establish accountability reporting, analytical care and usable visual explanation. A large archive can exist elsewhere, but it should not determine the homepage hierarchy.

**Ask:** What claim about yourself can your current projects actually support?

**Expected responses:** For example, I can investigate local spending with records and explain the limits. Avoid expert or investigative specialist unless the evidence warrants it.

**Misconception:** Editing means hiding all imperfect work. It means choosing relevant evidence and honestly labelling its status.

**Transition:** Turn the thesis into five decisions.



## 4. Five principles of a strong portfolio

06:00–09:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Apply five editorial tests to a homepage.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Clarity: write health and public-service reporter rather than story alchemist. Selection: lead with a completed district-health analysis rather than every class notebook. Evidence: link the data and describe a finding instead of calling yourself rigorous. Readability: a readable headline should survive a small phone. Attribution: say you built the map for a team story rather than implying you reported every interview. Each choice changes what a reader can reasonably infer.

**Ask:** Which principle would most improve your current site?

**Expected responses:** Ask for a specific change: identify my map contribution, choose my strongest story or replace my generic introduction.

**Misconception:** Being clear means having no personality. Distinct reporting interests and specific work provide personality.

**Transition:** Show these decisions in two constructed homepages.



## 5. Two portfolios, two impressions

09:00–13:00 · 4 min



**Objective:** Read homepage design as a set of editorial priorities.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Both homepages are original fictional teaching mockups, not screenshots of real people. The left stands in for an oversized animated introduction; we do not actually animate it. Its ten numbered thumbnails say nothing about the reporting. On the right, an editorial identity, three distinguishable projects and role labels let a visitor evaluate the work. Contact is visible. Ask students to identify consequences before suggesting prettier styles.

**Ask:** What can you infer about each journalist, and what evidence supports that inference?

**Expected responses:** The right makes a reporting interest and contribution visible. The left may contain strong work but withholds the information needed to judge it.

**Misconception:** The right proves its reporting is accurate. A homepage can expose evidence; readers still need to inspect the work.

**Transition:** Apply the same reading test to your own homepage.



## 6. The ten-second test

13:00–15:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Turn a vague impression into four observable questions.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Give students ten seconds to imagine their current homepage, then ask one volunteer what a stranger would learn. This is a quick diagnostic, not a speed-reading competition. Read the title, visible work and contact route rather than judging taste. A site can pass without a custom domain or elaborate logo.

**Ask:** What information would you move above the fold?

**Expected responses:** The reporting identity, strongest project headline or a direct contact link. It depends on what is currently missing.

**Misconception:** Everything must fit on the first screen. The first screen should orient; further detail can follow.

**Transition:** Choose the work that deserves that prominent space.



## 7. Three strong projects beat fifteen weak ones

15:00–17:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Distinguish quality, publication status and technical ambition.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Three is a useful starting constraint, not a universal hiring rule. A student assignment can be strong evidence if clearly labelled and complete. An experiment can demonstrate a technique without claiming original reporting. A published article can be weak for this portfolio if it does not support the direction the student wants to show. Never imply that an unpublished exercise was commissioned by a newsroom.

**Ask:** Can an experiment sit beside a reported story?

**Expected responses:** Yes, if its status, contribution and purpose are explicit. Do not make the reader discover that a demonstration contains invented data.

**Misconception:** Only paid published work belongs in a portfolio. Credible student work can demonstrate ability.

**Transition:** Make a selection with incomplete and uneven options.



## 8. Which three make the cut?

17:00–21:00 · 4 min



**Objective:** Prioritise significance, rigour and completion over novelty.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Take ninety seconds for pairs to choose, then compare two selections. The municipal story can demonstrate reporting; district health can demonstrate reproducibility; the election map can demonstrate explanation if complete and tested. These are fictional descriptions, so the titles do not prove quality. Ask what evidence students would inspect before choosing. The calculator might be useful in a service-journalism portfolio, but does not automatically beat completed public-interest reporting.

**Ask:** What would have to change for the unfinished scraper to displace one of your choices?

**Expected responses:** A relevant question, reliable output, verification, clear status and an explanation of what it adds. Technical difficulty alone is insufficient.

**Misconception:** There is one correct set based on project titles. Actual quality, role and target audience decide.

**Transition:** Describe a selected project so that these judgments become possible.



## 9. How to describe a project

21:00–23:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Present a project as a finding and a contribution rather than a stack.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Explain each field with the municipal story: a precise headline, one central finding, responsibility for records/interviews/analysis, source and method notes, then a working story link. Code is useful where it clarifies or enables reproduction; not every reporting piece needs a repository. Distinguish a link to output from a link to supporting evidence.

**Ask:** What is missing from “Python, Pandas, charts”?

**Expected responses:** The reporting question, result, significance, personal role and limitations.

**Misconception:** Every card needs a long abstract. A short description can orient; method detail belongs behind a clear link.

**Transition:** Compare two descriptions of the same fictional analysis.



## 10. Weak description versus strong description

23:00–26:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Show how specificity and restraint make a description more credible.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** This rainfall example is fictional; no trend finding is being asserted about actual districts. The stronger description names question, method, result and limit. Five years alone may be insufficient to support a climate trend claim: the wording records what this limited analysis found, not a conclusion about long-term climate. In a real portfolio, verify what test was used and whether variability versus annual levels was actually analysed. Improve the method wording if it misdescribes the work.

**Ask:** Which words would you challenge before publishing this description?

**Expected responses:** Increasing variability needs a defined measure, appropriate timeframe and test. “No consistent increase” is not proof of no climate change. The source, years and limits should be available.

**Misconception:** A null or mixed result is not portfolio-worthy. Careful findings can demonstrate judgment.

**Transition:** Now make the author’s personal role equally precise.



## 11. What exactly was your contribution?

26:00–28:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Make collaboration and AI assistance legible without inflated claims.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Use a team investigation to separate reporting, analysis, design, editing and engineering. Naming collaborators adds credibility because the role can be evaluated. Describe AI help at the level that matters: generated layout/debugging assistance does not establish who checked a number or interviewed a source. Follow the publisher’s disclosure policy; do not claim independent verification unless it happened.

**Ask:** What if you adapted someone else’s open-source map?

**Expected responses:** Credit the original, respect its licence and name your own adaptation, data work and reporting. A public repository is not automatically permission to copy.

**Misconception:** Honest collaboration makes you look less capable. It helps a reader know exactly what you can do.

**Transition:** Check whether the portfolio itself meets your reporting standards.



## 12. A portfolio is also a fact-checking exercise

28:00–30:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Apply evidence standards to professional self-presentation.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Open a project description and check each claim against the work. If it says five years, locate all five years. If it says built, specify the contribution. Confirm the published link works without your login. Do not expose confidential sources, restricted data or personal information just to demonstrate rigour; offer an appropriate public method summary instead.

**Ask:** Which portfolio sentence is stronger than your underlying evidence?

**Expected responses:** I proved, built everything, expert or independently verified may need narrowing. Ask for an example from the student’s actual text.

**Misconception:** Fact-checking only applies to the underlying story, not the promotional copy.

**Transition:** Build the smallest structure that makes the verified work readable.



## 13. The minimum viable journalism portfolio

30:00–32:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Give students an achievable responsive information structure.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Walk from name and identity to a two-sentence introduction, selected work, short About and contact. GitHub and LinkedIn serve different evidence and contact needs; email should not require a puzzle. A CV is optional and should be current and accessible. On mobile these projects stack in a meaningful order. The wireframe is original placeholder content, not a real person’s site.

**Ask:** What can move off the homepage?

**Expected responses:** A full biography, exhaustive archive, long technical methods and a detailed CV can live behind clear links.

**Misconception:** Minimum viable means unfinished or careless. It means sufficient, coherent and tested.

**Transition:** Fill the most important blank: the introduction.



## 14. Write an introduction that says something

32:00–34:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Describe a direction without inventing expertise.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** The stronger example names a beat, purpose and approach. It is a claim students should adapt to their actual work. A student interested in culture can be just as specific without copying policy language. “Interested in” honestly distinguishes a direction from a track record; a linked piece should show how the approach has begun.

**Ask:** Which phrase in your introduction could belong to every applicant?

**Expected responses:** Passionate storyteller, hardworking, creative or enthusiastic. Replace one with a question, beat or demonstrated practice.

**Misconception:** Specificity means declaring a permanent career niche. A current direction can change as the work develops.

**Transition:** Give the introduction a concrete piece of evidence.



## 15. A project card that earns a click

34:00–37:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Integrate purpose, evidence, role and access in one readable entry.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** This original chart uses fictional values 80%, 55% and 30%, shown on one common percentage scale. The headline would require reporting about delays, not just low expenditure. Explain that budgets, actual spending and physical completion are different measures. The card names documents and interviews because the story claim needs both. In a student’s actual site, replace the demonstration labels with real story and method links.

**Ask:** Does low spending alone establish why repairs were delayed?

**Expected responses:** No. Check accounting periods, implementation records, procurement and interviews. The description must match the actual reporting.

**Misconception:** An attractive chart supplies the whole explanation. It is one part of the evidence.

**Transition:** The publishing stack can remain much simpler than the reporting.



## 16. You already know enough to build this

37:00–39:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Remove technical overreach from the immediate task.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Do not reteach syntax. Students have already published stories. A portfolio connects selected evidence and should not become a new application project. Start from files they understand and can maintain. Frameworks are options when a real need arises; they are not credentials. GitHub Free supports public Pages repositories, so remind students that published files and repository history may be public.

**Ask:** What feature would make the portfolio better for its reader today?

**Expected responses:** Clearer project links, readable mobile text or accessible contact, not necessarily an interactive introduction.

**Misconception:** A simple stack signals weak technical ability. Thoughtful reporting and maintainable implementation are stronger evidence.

**Transition:** Use the existing publishing knowledge in a short, accurate workflow.



## 17. Publish with GitHub Pages

39:00–42:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Publish plain files without confusing source and deployment.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** For this starter, put index.html and style.css in the repository root, not a nested starter folder. Use main if that is the branch containing the files; another existing branch can also be selected. GitHub offers root or /docs for branch publishing. You need repository admin or maintainer permissions to configure it. A normal project site uses https://USERNAME.github.io/REPOSITORY/; a user site uses a repository named USERNAME.github.io and publishes at the domain root. Keep starter asset paths relative so both work. Custom Actions builds are possible but unnecessary here. Check the Pages deployment status if the first request returns 404; verify branch, folder, filename case and index.html. Never paste credentials or restricted files into a public repo.

**Ask:** Why might a stylesheet work locally but fail on a project site?

**Expected responses:** A root-relative /style.css can point outside the repository path; use style.css for this plain starter and check filename case.

**Misconception:** Pushing files always enables Pages automatically. The publishing source still needs configuration.

**Transition:** Treat deployment as the start of reader testing, not the end.



## 18. Test it like a reader, not its creator

42:00–45:00 · 3 min



**Objective:** Test real reading tasks across screen sizes.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** These are original schematic demonstrations. Use a volunteer’s actual page for the live test if available. Open it on a real phone or narrow preview; ask someone else to find the strongest project and contact link. Tab through links, check visible focus, text contrast and missing alt text. Test each destination signed out where feasible. Optimise oversized images and give them dimensions; a screenshot should not replace a real text headline. Avoid hover-only menus. Publishing can expose a problem that local preview hides.

**Ask:** What did a fresh reader fail to find?

**Expected responses:** A headline, role, working story or contact path. Identify the observable failure before changing colours.

**Misconception:** A responsive site is simply a scaled-down desktop screenshot.

**Transition:** Move from demonstration to a tangible first version.



## 19. Your assignment: build the first version

45:00–46:00 · 1 min



**Objective:** Launch a time-bounded task that ends with a usable draft.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** This launch minute is part of the twelve-minute build block; the next slide holds eleven minutes of work. Ask students to use their existing repository if it reduces friction. Set a visible timer. Tell students the output is an introduction, three chosen entries and one improved description, with a live update if feasible. A publication failure should not consume the full clinic: keep a local draft and record the deployment issue. Do not invent a finding or turn a placeholder into a false link.

**Ask:** What will you have at minute twelve?

**Expected responses:** A draft homepage and at least one specific project description, ideally published. Students with fewer finished pieces label remaining entries WIP.

**Misconception:** Completing a theme or custom domain is required before the work can be evaluated.

**Transition:** Open the starter only if it helps you finish faster.



## 20. A starting template, not a finished identity

46:00–57:00 · 11 min



**Objective:** Support independent editing with a dependency-free starting point.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Facilitation: minute 1 choose projects and identity; minutes 2–4 write the introduction and strongest project; minutes 5–8 replace template content or update the existing page; minutes 9–10 publish if ready; minute 11 open the result on a phone and prepare the link. Give a two-minute warning. Circulate first to students without an output, then to broken publishing workflows. Download and extract the ZIP; copy its contents to the root of a student’s own portfolio repository. The starter is a separate plain HTML/CSS directory with no dependencies. Its project slots are explicitly unfinished and have no invented story links. Replace placeholders and add actual href values only when work exists. Do not let students upload the teaching deck or assume the starter biography is theirs.

**Ask:** What if I only have one finished piece?

**Expected responses:** Write it accurately, then label two future entries as WIP with intended questions. Do not pretend they are reported or published.

**Misconception:** A starter confers an identity. Every editorial claim still needs the student’s choice and evidence.

**Transition:** At the timer, exchange links even if the draft is imperfect.



## 21. Peer review: be the hiring editor

57:00–61:00 · 4 min



**Objective:** Use an unfamiliar reader to test the argument rather than the author’s intentions.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Pair students; each person reviews the other site concurrently for four minutes. First thirty seconds: read silently without the author explaining. Next ninety seconds: inspect the first project, role and method access. Next minute: use a narrow phone view and find contact. Final minute: write one observation plus one recommended edit. If a site is not live, review the local draft side by side and note that public access remains untested. Do not ask students to send personal contact details to the whole class.

**Ask:** What did you infer, and where did the page support it?

**Expected responses:** Report the reader’s inference with a specific element. “I assumed you reported this because the role is missing” is more actionable than “nice design”.

**Misconception:** Peer review is a verdict on talent or a vote for favourite fonts.

**Transition:** Record what the page demonstrates and choose a revision.



## 22. The portfolio scorecard

61:00–63:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Make evaluation observable without pretending to measure employability.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Give reviewers ninety seconds to mark only what they actually observed, then thirty seconds to identify the most consequential unchecked item. A checked mobile item requires actually inspecting the narrow layout. “Descriptions communicate findings” can include an honest purpose for an unfinished experiment. The count is a facilitation aid, not a psychometric scale or grade. No entries are stored or sent anywhere.

**Ask:** Which unchecked item prevents a reader from judging the work?

**Expected responses:** Missing role, unclear finding or inaccessible output often matters before spacing. Ask for the evidence behind a check.

**Misconception:** Six ticks prove the reporting is true or that the candidate should be hired.

**Transition:** Make the highest-impact change while feedback is fresh.



## 23. The one edit that matters most

63:00–65:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Prioritise an editorial revision that can be completed now.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Ask each student to write the chosen edit in one sentence and spend ninety seconds making it. Use the last thirty seconds to verify the revised public or local page. If a link is broken, fixing access may outrank rewriting. If the problem is an unsupported claim, narrow it before any cosmetic work. Invite one quick before-and-after example if time permits; avoid redesigning a volunteer’s entire site.

**Ask:** What can a reader understand now that they could not understand before?

**Expected responses:** An actual finding, accurate role, meaningful project order or usable access. Require a concrete difference.

**Misconception:** High impact means a large visual change. A single sentence can repair the portfolio’s central claim.

**Transition:** Keep this revision habit after the course ends.



## 24. The course ends. The learning doesn’t.

65:00–66:00 · 1 min



**Objective:** Move from assigned tasks to self-directed editorial decisions.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Independent project selection is a professional skill: find a public-interest question with obtainable evidence, scope it so it can finish and set a deadline. Students need not learn every library before publishing. Look for a small question on a beat they already understand. Accountability includes deciding when data cannot answer it.

**Ask:** What question could you begin without waiting for permission?

**Expected responses:** A specific local service, budget or access question with an identifiable source and audience.

**Misconception:** Learning independently means working alone or never asking for help.

**Transition:** Link methods to a project that creates a reason to learn.



## 25. Stop collecting tutorials. Start finishing projects.

66:00–68:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Connect learning to retrieval, adaptation and a finished output.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Do not dismiss tutorials: they help with a specific skill, but copying a finished notebook is not independent analysis. Reproduce one small example, close it and apply the method to the reporting question. Keep a short log of assumptions, failures and verification. Publication can be a modest chart with methods, not always a large investigation. Reflection asks which decision changed the finding.

**Ask:** How would you know you learned more than the keystrokes?

**Expected responses:** Explain the method, adapt it, test a failure case and identify when it does not apply.

**Misconception:** Finishing means publishing everything regardless of evidence. Sometimes the responsible output is a methods note explaining why a claim failed.

**Transition:** Choose a sustainable sequence of small outputs.



## 26. Your next six months

68:00–70:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Make learning measurable and achievable without prescribing a single career path.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** This is a sample roadmap, not a required monthly output quota. Month two is a credited reproduction, not original reporting; compare methods and identify differences rather than copying a screenshot. Month three should be tightly scoped. Month four’s technique could be geocoding or scraping only if the question requires it. Month five can be a documented bug fix or analysis contribution, with permission and attribution. Month six revisits the edited argument by replacing a weaker entry. Reduce scope around work or care commitments.

**Ask:** Which step is least feasible for you, and what smaller version preserves the learning?

**Expected responses:** A one-chart reproduction, one-district story or documentation contribution can be realistic. A schedule should include verification and revision.

**Misconception:** Progress means continually adding new tools and never revisiting old work.

**Transition:** Find resources that fit different parts of that process.



## 27. Where to learn and find inspiration

70:00–72:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Distinguish learning a method, studying a story, reading code and meeting peers.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** These links were checked for this class. DataJournalism.com offers courses and handbooks; use a relevant chapter. Source documents newsroom work and supports the OpenNews community; look for decisions and tradeoffs. Study The Pudding for how form follows subject, not a style to clone. Datawrapper Academy explains practical visualisation tasks; its blog adds design discussions. GitHub hosts many kinds of projects: inspect licences, data, README, commit context and reproducibility before relying on code. Community is reciprocal—ask specific questions, credit help and share a useful finding. Do not assume public code is verified or licensed for unrestricted reuse.

**Ask:** Which resource would help with a confusing map label versus an unfamiliar analysis repository?

**Expected responses:** Academy for the visualisation problem; the repository’s README, code and methods for implementation. Source/OpenNews can show newsroom decisions and peer context.

**Misconception:** An inspiring interactive or popular repository is automatically a suitable method or a trustworthy source.

**Transition:** Use AI inside this learning process with a clear responsibility boundary.



## 28. What AI changes, and what it doesn’t

72:00–73:00 · 1 min



**Objective:** Maintain accountability while using assistance productively.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Connect to the class’s own AI use. A generated HTML layout may save time, but an invented citation or silent change to a denominator remains the journalist’s problem. Ask AI to explain code and propose checks; then inspect outputs against source records and meaningful tests. Do not submit confidential material without permission. Your role description should say what you verified and what assistance you used when relevant to the publication’s policy.

**Ask:** What would you verify before adding an AI-assisted project to your portfolio?

**Expected responses:** Claims, sources, transformations, labels, accessibility and personal contribution—not merely whether code runs.

**Misconception:** The tool’s confidence or a second model’s agreement supplies independent verification.

**Transition:** Close with a submission that joins public evidence to a learning commitment.



## 29. Final assignment and closing

73:00–75:00 · 2 min



**Objective:** Close with two concrete deliverables rather than a motivational abstraction.

**Say / demonstrate / facilitate:** Ask students to submit their live URL and a short six-month plan through the course’s usual submission channel; no deadline or channel has been invented here. Three entries can include clearly labelled WIP, but finished work must link to actual output. The plan should connect each skill to a reporting question, name two original ideas with potential sources, and include feasible publishing and verification dates. Invite one student to name their first next action. End on the craft: tools change, but evidence, questions and explanation remain the work.

**Ask:** What is the first action you will take after leaving this room?

**Expected responses:** A publishable scope, a verified source to inspect, a specific portfolio correction or a realistic date—not “learn everything”.

**Misconception:** The final submission freezes the portfolio permanently. It is the first edited version of an ongoing practice.

**Transition:** Thank the class and invite them to keep revising as the work improves.
